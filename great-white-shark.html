Great White Shark

Shark in a Snapshot

Great white sharks are often feared and earned a reputation as some of the most dangerous creatures in the ocean. However, beneath this fearsome exterior lies a fascinating and complex species that plays a crucial role in maintaining the health of marine ecosystems. As one of my favourite shark species, great white sharks fascinate me because, despite their fearsome reputation, they are incredibly intelligent and strategic hunters, with an extraordinary sense of smell. They can detect a single drop of blood from up to a third of a mile away and even smell a seal colony from two miles away.

Fin-tastic Facts

Great white sharks have an incredible body design that makes them supreme ocean hunters. They are torpedo-shaped with powerful tails that can propel them through water at up to 15 miles per hour. Their body is strategically coloured – grey or brown on top to blend with ocean depths, and white underneath to match sunlight on the water's surface, allowing perfect camouflage.

They have an astonishing 300 serrated, triangular teeth that can reach up to 6.6 inches in height. Their ancestry dates back more than 400 million years, predating dinosaurs and they can survive a month or two without another big meal. Despite being often considered solitary, great white sharks actually have a social hierarchy where females typically dominate males. Interestingly, they are generally conflict-avoidant and rarely resort to combat with each other or humans.

Encounters of the Deep

White sharks do not intentionally attack humans as prey. Instead, they may bite humans for several reasons:

  • Sharks use their mouths to investigate unfamiliar objects in their environment, so they are known to take "test bites." These exploratory bites can cause significant damage due to the shark's powerful jaws and sharp teeth, even though they are not meant to be fatal.
  • A common reason for shark bites occurs when they mistake humans on surfboards or swimming for seals or sea lions. From below, the two can look quite similar, especially in murky waters or at a distance.
  • Sharks can detect electrical signals from muscle movement, which may lead them to investigate potential prey.
  • As apex predators, white sharks have little fear of other creatures and may approach unfamiliar objects or organisms out of curiosity.
  • Some shark bites may occur due to territorial reasons or as a way of establishing dominance over their area.

It's important to note that shark bites on humans are rare, and white sharks typically do not consume humans after biting them. Most incidents involve a single bite, after which the shark leaves, suggesting that humans are not their intended prey. Most shark species primarily feed on smaller fish, invertebrates, and marine mammals like seals and sea lions, as they prefer high-fat prey, and humans are relatively lean and bony.

According to Keartes (n.d.), sharks are responsible for an average of 10 human fatalities per year, while fisheries kill approximately 100 million sharks annually. To put this into perspective, Keartes also highlights that elephants cause around 600 deaths per year, and ants are responsible for 30 fatalities annually.

Endangered or Thriving?

White sharks are not technically endangered, but they are vulnerable to extinction. They are currently listed as "Vulnerable" on the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species—one category above endangered, but still facing a high risk of extinction. It is estimated 80% population decline in North America over 14 years (Nautilus, 2018).

There are several reasons for their current vulnerability, which includes slow reproductive cycle (female sharks take 35 years to reach sexual maturity), limited reproduction (2-10 pups every 2-3 years) and frequent human threats likes sport fishing, fin & teeth trade, trophy hunting, and bycatch in commercial fishing.
